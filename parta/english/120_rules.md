# PART 1: PRONOUNS AND DETERMINERS

## 1. Each vs Every

### Meaning
- **Each** focuses on individuals separately.
- **Every** considers all members of a group.

### Rules

1. **Each** can be used for two or more people or things.
2. **Every** is generally used for more than two members.
3. Each can be a pronoun or a determiner/adjective.
4. Every is used as a determiner before a noun, not independently as a pronoun.
5. Each, every, either and neither generally take a singular verb when they are the subject.
6. A noun directly following each/every is singular.
7. In constructions such as `each of the students`, the noun after `of the` is plural, but the main verb is singular.

### Examples

- Each student **is** ready.
- Every student **is** ready.
- Each of the students **has** a book.
- Every one of my friends **is** helpful.
- I have a book in **each hand**.

Incorrect: Every of the students is ready.

Correct: Every student is ready.

Correct: Each of the students is ready.

### Exam trick

Check three things:

- Is the word followed directly by a noun?
- Is it followed by `of the`?
- Does the subject require a singular verb?

**Remember:** Each/every + singular verb.

---

## 2. Both

### Rule 1: Both ... and

Use `and` to connect two people, objects or ideas with both.

- Both Ram **and** Shyam are present.
- Both English **and** Mathematics are important.

Incorrect: Both Ram as well as Shyam are present.

### Rule 2: Avoid both ... not

For the meaning “neither of the two,” use `neither ... nor`.

- Neither Ram nor Shyam **is** present.
- Neither the teacher nor the student **was** late.

Incorrect: Both Ram and Shyam are not present.  
For a clear negative meaning, prefer: Neither Ram nor Shyam is present.

### Rule 3: Position of possessives

Place a possessive before the noun phrase introduced by both.

- Both my brothers are engineers.
- Both her parents are teachers.

Incorrect: My both brothers are engineers.

### Memory trick

**Both → AND**  
**Neither → NOR**

---

## 3. Either and Neither

- **Either** = one or the other of two.
- **Neither** = not one and not the other.

Examples:

- Either answer **is** acceptable.
- Neither answer **is** correct.
- Either of the two candidates **has** a chance.
- Neither of the students **was** absent.

When either/neither joins two subjects using `or/nor`, the verb generally agrees with the nearer subject in traditional exam grammar.

- Either the teacher or the students **are** coming.
- Either the students or the teacher **is** coming.

### Exam trick

Identify the structure:

- `Either + singular noun` → singular verb.
- `Neither + singular noun` → singular verb.
- `Either ... or` / `Neither ... nor` → check the nearer subject.

---

## 4. Who vs Whom

### Subjective and objective pronouns

| Subjective case | Objective case |
|---|---|
| I | Me |
| We | Us |
| He | Him |
| She | Her |
| They | Them |
| Who | Whom |

**Who** performs the action or acts as the subject.

**Whom** receives the action or follows a preposition as its object.

Examples:

- The person **who** called me is my friend.
- The person **whom** I called is my friend.
- To **whom** did you give the book?
- The girl **who** won the prize is my sister.

### The replacement trick

Replace the blank with `he/she/they` or `him/her/them`.

- If **he/she/they** fits, use **who**.
- If **him/her/them** fits, use **whom**.

Example:

The man ___ helped me is a doctor.

“He helped me” works → **who**.

The man ___ I helped is a doctor.

“I helped him” works → **whom**.

---

## 5. Whoever vs Whomever

Use whoever when the word functions as a subject within its clause.

Use whomever when it functions as an object within its clause.

- Invite **whoever** is interested.
- Give the prize to **whomever** you select.

### Exam trick

Do not decide only by looking at the main sentence. Check the grammatical role of the word inside its own clause.

---

## 6. Which, Who and That

### Which

Traditionally used for things and, in some constructions, animals.

- The book **which** I bought is useful.
- The dog **which** was injured received treatment.

### Who

Used for people.

- The student **who** won the prize is brilliant.

### That

Can refer to people, animals or things in many defining relative clauses.

- The student **that** won the prize is brilliant.
- The book **that** I bought is useful.
- The dog **that** barked belongs to my neighbour.

### High-yield rule: That after certain expressions

In many defining relative clauses, **that** is preferred after expressions such as:

- The same
- The only
- All
- Everything
- Nothing
- Anything
- Something
- Superlatives such as the best, the most important

Examples:

- This is the same book **that** I read.
- This is the best answer **that** I can give.
- All **that** glitters is not gold.

**Important:** Do not apply this as an absolute rule to every sentence. Grammar depends on the construction.

---

## 7. Pronoun Agreement

A pronoun should agree with its antecedent—the noun or expression to which it refers.

Examples:

- Every student must bring **his or her** identity card.
- Every student must bring **their** identity card. (Common and accepted in modern English.)

In traditional competitive-exam questions, singular pronouns may be expected after each/every, but always consider the wording and the exam's prescribed grammar convention.

Incorrect: Each student must submit their forms, if only one form per student is intended and the exam requires strict traditional agreement.

Better: Each student must submit a form.

### Quick check

Ask:

1. Which noun does the pronoun refer to?
2. Is that noun singular or plural?
3. Does the pronoun make the meaning clear?

---

## 8. Reciprocal Pronouns: Each Other and One Another

These indicate a mutual action or relationship.

- The two friends helped **each other**.
- The team members supported **one another**.

Traditional textbook distinction:

- Each other → two
- One another → more than two

In modern English, this distinction is not always strictly maintained. Follow the convention used in the exam or textbook.

Incorrect: I beg your pardon of you.

Correct: I beg your pardon.

---

## 9. Personal Pronouns: Person and Order

### Three grammatical persons

- First person: I, we
- Second person: you
- Third person: he, she, it, they

Traditional exam convention for a neutral or positive statement:

**Second person + third person + first person**

Example: You, he and I will attend the meeting.

For an admission of fault, traditional school grammar often teaches:

**First person + second person + third person**

Example: I, you and he made a mistake.

### Exam tip

Treat these as traditional examination conventions rather than universal rules for every context. Natural English also depends on emphasis and sentence meaning.

---

## 10. Possessive Adjectives and Pronouns

### Possessive adjectives

my, our, your, his, her, its, their

They come before nouns:

- This is **my book**.
- That is **their house**.

### Possessive pronouns

mine, ours, yours, his, hers, theirs

They stand independently:

- This book is **mine**.
- That house is **theirs**.

Incorrect: This book is my.

Correct: This book is mine.

### Memory trick

**My + noun**  
**Mine without a noun immediately following it**

---

## 11. Apostrophe + s ('s)

The possessive form can show ownership or association.

- Ram's car
- The teacher's book
- The student's notebook

Plural noun ending in `s`:

- The students' classroom
- The teachers' meeting

Irregular plural:

- The children's toys
- The men's room

### Common exam trap

Do not add an apostrophe to possessive pronouns.

Correct: yours, hers, ours, theirs, its

Incorrect: your's, her's, our's, their's, it's (when possession is intended)

**It's** = it is / it has  
**Its** = possessive form

# PART 2: VERBS AND TENSES

## 12. Subject–Verb Agreement

### Basic rule

A singular subject generally takes a singular verb; a plural subject generally takes a plural verb.

- He **plays** cricket.
- They **play** cricket.
- The student **is** ready.
- The students **are** ready.

### Each, every, either, neither

- Each student **has** a notebook.
- Every candidate **is** ready.
- Neither answer **is** correct.

### Indefinite pronouns

Words such as everyone, everybody, someone, somebody, anyone, nobody and each are generally singular in traditional agreement.

- Everyone **is** present.
- Nobody **knows** the answer.

### Exam trick

Do not let a noun between the subject and verb confuse you.

- The quality of the apples **is** good.
- The list of candidates **is** ready.

The real subject is `quality` and `list`, not apples or candidates.

---

## 13. Collective Nouns

A collective noun names a group: team, family, committee, audience, class.

When the group acts as one unit, use a singular verb in standard American usage and often in formal exam grammar.

- The team **is** winning.
- The committee **has** made its decision.

When individual members are emphasised, plural agreement may occur in British English.

- The team **are** arguing among themselves.

### Exam tip

For a traditional competitive-exam question, check whether the group is treated as one unit or as separate members. Follow the convention used by the exam.

---

## 14. Stative Verbs

Some verbs describe states, thoughts, feelings, possession or perception. They are not normally used in continuous forms when they express those states.

Common examples:

- know
- believe
- understand
- belong
- own
- seem
- prefer
- need

Examples:

Correct: I **know** the answer.

Incorrect in the usual meaning: I am knowing the answer.

Correct: She **owns** a car.

Incorrect in the usual meaning: She is owning a car.

Some verbs can be continuous when their meaning changes:

- I **think** you are right. (opinion)
- I **am thinking** about the problem. (mental activity)

### Memory trick

If the verb describes a state rather than an ongoing action, check whether the continuous tense is appropriate.

---

## 15. Present Perfect and Time Expressions

Words frequently associated with the present perfect include:

- already
- just
- yet
- so far
- ever
- never
- recently

Examples:

- I have **already finished** my work.
- She has **just arrived**.
- Have you finished **yet**?
- I have never seen this film.

However, these words do not automatically require the present perfect in every context. The time reference and meaning matter.

### Past point of time

When a completed action is linked to a definite finished time in the past, use the simple past.

- I met him yesterday.
- She submitted the form last week.
- They arrived two hours ago.

Incorrect: I have met him yesterday.

Correct: I met him yesterday.

---

## 16. Since vs For

Both can describe duration in a continuing situation.

### Since = starting point

- since Monday
- since 2020
- since morning
- since I joined the school

### For = period of time

- for two hours
- for three days
- for five years

Examples:

- I have lived here **since 2020**.
- I have lived here **for six years**.

### Memory trick

**Since = when it started**  
**For = how long**

---

## 17. Ago vs Before

### Ago

Counts backwards from the present.

- I met him two days **ago**.
- She left an hour **ago**.

### Before

Usually relates one time or event to another time or event.

- He had left before I arrived.
- I had seen that film before.

### Exam trick

- One finished action with a time counted from now → often **ago + simple past**.
- One past action earlier than another past action → often **before + past perfect/simple past**, depending on meaning.

---

## 18. Past Perfect

When two events happened in the past, the earlier event may take the past perfect.

**Earlier event:** had + V3  
**Later event:** simple past

- The train **had left** before we reached the station.
- She **had finished** her work when I arrived.

The past perfect is not required simply because an event happened in the past. Use it when the earlier-than-another-past-time relationship needs to be expressed.

---

## 19. Conditional Sentences

A conditional sentence contains a condition and a result.

### Zero conditional

Used for general truths and regular results.

**If + simple present, simple present**

- If you heat ice, it melts.
- If water reaches its boiling point under standard atmospheric pressure, it boils.

### First conditional

Used for a realistic future possibility.

**If + simple present, will + V1**

- If I study, I will pass.
- If it rains, we will stay indoors.

### Second conditional

Used for an unreal or hypothetical present/future situation.

**If + simple past, would + V1**

- If I were rich, I would travel the world.
- If I knew the answer, I would tell you.

Traditional exam grammar commonly uses **were** for hypothetical situations with all subjects.

### Third conditional

Used for an unreal past condition and its imagined result.

**If + past perfect, would have + V3**

- If I had studied, I would have passed.
- If she had left earlier, she would have caught the train.

### Golden table

| Condition | Result |
|---|---|
| If + V1 / simple present | will/can/may + V1 |
| If + V2 / simple past | would/could/might + V1 |
| If + had + V3 | would/could/might have + V3 |

### Exam trap

Avoid using `will` in the if-clause of a normal first conditional.

Incorrect: If I will study, I will pass.

Correct: If I study, I will pass.

**Exception:** `Will` can appear in an if-clause when it expresses willingness or insistence, not an ordinary future condition.

---

## 20. Inversion of Sentences

Inversion means changing the normal word order, often placing an auxiliary verb before the subject.

Normal: I have never seen such a scene.

Inversion: Never **have I seen** such a scene.

Words that can trigger inversion when placed at the beginning include:

- Never
- Rarely
- Seldom
- Hardly
- Scarcely
- No sooner
- Not only
- Little (in certain negative or restrictive uses)

### No sooner ... than

- No sooner **had** I reached the station **than** the train arrived.

### Hardly/scarcely ... when

- Hardly **had** I reached the station **when** the train arrived.
- Scarcely **had** she entered **when** the phone rang.

### Exam memory trick

- **No sooner → than**
- **Hardly/scarcely/barely → when**

Watch for the auxiliary verb before the subject.

---

## 21. Time Clauses with Hardly, Scarcely and Barely

When these expressions describe one event happening almost immediately before another, the usual examination patterns are:

- Hardly had + subject + V3 + when ...
- Scarcely had + subject + V3 + when ...
- No sooner had + subject + V3 + than ...

Incorrect: No sooner had he arrived when it started raining.

Correct: No sooner had he arrived than it started raining.

---

## 22. Used to, Be Used to and Get Used to

These structures are easily confused.

- **Used to + V1**: a past habit or state that no longer applies.
- **Be used to + noun/gerund**: be accustomed to something.
- **Get used to + noun/gerund**: become accustomed to something.

Examples:

- I **used to play** cricket.
- I **am used to waking** up early.
- She is **getting used to living** in Delhi.

### Exam trick

After `be used to` and `get used to`, the word `to` is a preposition, so a noun or gerund can follow it.

# PART 3: CONJUNCTIONS AND CONNECTORS

## 23. Although, Though, Even Though and But

Use although/though to introduce a contrast.

- Although he is rich, he is unhappy.
- Though she worked hard, she failed.

Do not normally use `although ... but` together in the same simple clause structure.

Incorrect: Although he is poor, but he is honest.

Correct: Although he is poor, he is honest.

Also correct: He is poor, but he is honest.

### Exam trick

Choose one structure:

- Although + clause, clause
- Clause + but + clause

---

## 24. Despite and In Spite Of

These are followed by a noun phrase or gerund, not directly by a normal finite clause.

- Despite the rain, we played.
- In spite of being tired, she continued.
- Despite his illness, he attended the meeting.

To use a full clause, use `although`.

- Although he was tired, he continued.

Incorrect: Despite he was tired, he continued.

Correct: Despite being tired, he continued.

---

## 25. Both, Either, Neither and Not Only

### Both ... and

- Both Ram and Shyam came.

### Either ... or

- Either Ram or Shyam will come.

### Neither ... nor

- Neither Ram nor Shyam came.

### Not only ... but also

- She is not only intelligent but also hardworking.

### Agreement tip

With either/or and neither/nor, traditional competitive grammar generally uses the verb agreeing with the nearer subject.

- Neither the teachers nor the principal **is** present.
- Neither the principal nor the teachers **are** present.

---

## 26. As Well As, Together With, Along With

These expressions do not normally change the number of the main subject.

- Ram, as well as his friends, **is** coming.
- The teacher, along with the students, **was** present.

The verb agrees with the main subject before the expression.

### Exam trick

Ignore the phrase between commas temporarily and locate the main subject.

---

## 27. Lest

`Lest` means “for fear that” or “to avoid the possibility that.”

Traditional exam grammar often expects `should` or the base form after lest, depending on the construction.

- Work hard lest you **should fail**.
- Walk carefully lest you **fall**.

Avoid adding an unnecessary second negative such as `not` in a standard lest construction.

---

## 28. Unless

`Unless` means “if ... not.”

- Unless you study, you will not pass.
- Unless it rains, we will go outside.

Avoid unnecessary double negatives.

Incorrect: Unless you do not study, you will fail.

Better: Unless you study, you will fail.

Equivalent: If you do not study, you will fail.

---

## 29. Else

When else is used in comparisons, the standard pattern is often:

**else + than** in some fixed expressions, but **other than** is the common form for “different from.”

Examples:

- Nobody else **but** him knew the answer. (Construction-dependent)
- What else can I do?
- No one other than the principal can approve it.

For error detection, examine the whole sentence rather than automatically replacing every `than` after else. Fixed expressions and the intended meaning matter.

---

## 30. Words Commonly Used in Pairs

Learn common paired conjunctions and expressions:

- either ... or
- neither ... nor
- both ... and
- not only ... but also
- whether ... or
- no sooner ... than
- hardly/scarcely ... when
- such ... as
- so ... that

Examples:

- Whether you agree **or** disagree, be polite.
- It was **so** cold **that** we stayed inside.
- She is **not only** smart **but also** diligent.

### Memory trick

Whenever you see one member of a paired expression, check whether the matching member appears in the correct place.

---

# PART 4: ADJECTIVES AND COMPARISON

## 31. Positive Degree

Used to describe a quality without directly comparing two things.

- Ram is tall.
- The book is useful.

### Equality comparison

**as + adjective + as**

- Ram is as tall as Shyam.
- This question is as difficult as that one.

Negative form:

- Ram is not as tall as Shyam.

Incorrect: Ram is as taller as Shyam.

---

## 32. Comparative Degree

Used to compare two people or things.

- taller
- more useful
- better
- worse

Common structures:

- A is taller than B.
- This book is more useful than that one.
- She is better than I expected.

Avoid double comparison.

Incorrect: more better

Correct: better

Incorrect: more taller

Correct: taller

---

## 33. Superlative Degree

Used to indicate the highest or lowest degree within a group.

- the tallest
- the most useful
- the best
- the worst

Examples:

- She is the tallest girl in the class.
- This is the most important question.
- He is the best player on the team.

### Exam trick

Check whether the sentence compares:

- two items → often comparative
- three or more / an entire group → often superlative

This is a useful guide, not an absolute rule for every construction.

---

## 34. Double Comparatives and Superlatives

Avoid using two comparison markers for the same adjective.

Incorrect: more better  
Correct: better

Incorrect: most tallest  
Correct: tallest

Incorrect: more easier  
Correct: easier

---

## 35. Words Not Normally Compared

Certain adjectives already express an absolute or complete meaning in their ordinary use.

Examples often taught in traditional grammar:

- perfect
- unique
- excellent
- complete
- universal

In many formal exam questions, avoid expressions such as `more unique` or `most perfect` unless the context clearly uses the adjective in a relative or non-literal sense.

### Exam tip

Do not mechanically apply this rule to every word in every context. Contemporary English sometimes uses these adjectives comparatively for emphasis.

---

## 36. The + Comparative, The + Comparative

This structure shows that one change is linked to another.

- The harder you work, the better you perform.
- The more you practise, the more confident you become.
- The sooner we leave, the earlier we will arrive.

### Memory trick

**The + comparative ... the + comparative**

---

## 37. Times and Comparison

`Times` can express multiplication or a ratio of magnitude.

- This building is twice as high as that one.
- The new model costs three times as much as the old one.

Use the correct comparison pattern; avoid randomly adding a comparative adjective after `times`.

---

# PART 5: NOUNS AND COUNTABILITY

## 38. Countable and Uncountable Nouns

### Countable nouns

Can be counted individually:

- book/books
- student/students
- apple/apples

### Uncountable nouns

Usually treated as masses or concepts:

- information
- advice
- furniture
- luggage
- equipment
- knowledge

Examples:

Correct: a piece of advice

Incorrect in standard English: an advice

Correct: much information

Incorrect in standard English: many informations

---

## 39. Few, A Few and The Few

Used with countable plural nouns.

- **Few** = almost none; negative sense.
- **A few** = some; positive sense.
- **The few** = the small number that exists or is available.

Examples:

- Few students understood the question.
- A few students helped me.
- The few students who attended received certificates.

### Memory trick

**A few = some**  
**Few = almost none**  
**The few = those limited ones**

---

## 40. Little, A Little and The Little

Used with uncountable nouns.

- **Little** = almost none.
- **A little** = some.
- **The little** = the small amount that exists.

Examples:

- There is little hope.
- I have a little money.
- The little money he had was spent carefully.

### Quick comparison

| Countable | Uncountable |
|---|---|
| few | little |
| a few | a little |
| the few | the little |

---

## 41. Fewer vs Less

- **Fewer** is used with countable plural nouns.
- **Less** is used with uncountable quantities.

Examples:

- Fewer students attended.
- We need less water.
- There are fewer errors in this answer.
- I have less time today.

### Exam trap

If the noun can be counted individually, consider `fewer`.

If the noun expresses an amount or mass, consider `less`.

Some expressions involving measurements, money, time and distance conventionally use `less`.

---

## 42. Quantity and Number

- **Amount/quantity of** commonly refers to uncountable material or a measured quantity.
- **Number of** refers to countable items.

Examples:

- The amount of water is sufficient.
- The number of students is increasing.

### Memory trick

**Amount → mass/quantity**  
**Number → countable items**

---

## 43. Collective and Abstract Nouns

A collective noun names a group:

- team
- family
- committee
- audience

An abstract noun names a quality, feeling or idea:

- happiness
- honesty
- love
- laughter
- poverty
- reason

Use the word that matches the intended meaning and the grammatical construction.

---

## 44. Plurals of Compound Nouns

In many compound nouns, the main noun takes the plural ending.

Examples:

- mother-in-law → mothers-in-law
- passer-by → passers-by

For compounds ending in `-ful`, the plural is often formed by adding `s` at the end:

- handful → handfuls
- spoonful → spoonfuls
- cupful → cupfuls

### Exam trick

Do not automatically pluralise the first word of every compound. Identify the head noun or follow the established spelling.

---

# PART 6: PREPOSITIONS AND COMMON WORD USAGE

## 45. Prefer

Use **prefer A to B** when comparing two choices.

- I prefer tea to coffee.
- She prefers reading to watching television.

Incorrect in the standard comparison pattern: I prefer tea than coffee.

---

## 46. Angry With vs Angry At

Common patterns:

- angry **with** a person
- angry **at** a situation, action or behaviour

Examples:

- I am angry with him.
- She was angry at the delay.

Usage can vary with context; learn common collocations rather than treating them as absolute restrictions.

---

## 47. By vs With

### By

Often indicates the agent, means or method.

- The letter was written **by** Ram.
- I travel **by** bus.
- The report was prepared **by** the committee.

### With

Often indicates the instrument used.

- He cut the paper **with** scissors.
- She wrote with a pen.
- The dog was hit with a stick.

### Memory trick

**By = agent/method**  
**With = instrument**

Remember that many fixed expressions must be learned individually.

---

## 48. In, On and At

### In

Often used for enclosed spaces, areas, months, years and longer periods.

- in Delhi
- in a room
- in October
- in 2026

### On

Often used for surfaces, days and dates.

- on the table
- on Monday
- on 19 October

### At

Often used for specific points and clock times.

- at the bus stop
- at 9 p.m.
- at the entrance

### Memory trick

**At = point**  
**On = surface/day**  
**In = area/period**

This is a general guide; fixed expressions such as `at home`, `in bed` and `on time` need practice.

---

## 49. To + Verb vs To + Gerund

`To` can function as part of an infinitive or as a preposition.

### Infinitive

- I want **to study**.
- She decided **to leave**.

### Preposition

When `to` is a preposition, a noun or gerund may follow.

- I look forward to **meeting** you.
- She is accustomed to **working** late.
- He objected to **paying** the fee.

### Exam trick

Memorise common expressions followed by `to + V-ing`:

- look forward to
- be used to
- get used to
- object to
- be committed to

---

## 50. Verbs Followed by Particular Prepositions

Learn these common combinations:

- depend **on**
- insist **on**
- belong **to**
- consist **of**
- suffer **from**
- agree **with** a person
- agree **to** a proposal
- prefer A **to** B
- good **at**
- interested **in**
- capable **of**

### Exam strategy

For error detection, check the verb/adjective and its preposition as a unit. Many errors are collocation errors rather than tense errors.

---

## 51. In, Into, On and Onto

- `In` often indicates position inside something.
- `Into` often indicates movement from outside to inside.
- `On` often indicates position on a surface.
- `Onto` often indicates movement to a surface.

Examples:

- The keys are in the bag.
- She put the keys into the bag.
- The book is on the table.
- He placed the book onto the table.

---

# PART 7: OTHER HIGH-YIELD GRAMMAR TRAPS

## 52. Same, Only, All and Superlatives

In many defining relative clauses, `that` is preferred after expressions such as:

- the same
- the only
- all
- everything
- nothing
- the best / the most important

Examples:

- This is the same place **that** we visited.
- He is the only person **that** answered correctly.
- All **that** glitters is not gold.

Use the complete sentence to determine whether a defining or non-defining clause is intended.

---

## 53. Adjectives That Usually Do Not Take a Noun Directly

Some adjectives can be used without placing a noun immediately after them.

Examples:

- afraid
- asleep
- alive
- alone
- aware
- glad
- ill
- ready
- sure
- well
- worth

Correct:

- He is asleep.
- I am glad to hear that.
- The army is ready for action.
- The book is worth reading.

Incorrect: He is an asleep man.

Better: He is a man who is asleep.

### Important nuance

These words do not all behave identically. For example, `an ill man` is correct, while `an asleep man` is not standard. Learn each adjective's actual usage rather than applying one blanket rule.

---

## 54. Adjective vs Adverb

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun.

- She is a **careful** driver.

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective or another adverb.

- She drives **carefully**.
- He is **very** intelligent.
- She speaks **quite** clearly.

### Common errors

Incorrect: He runs quick.

Correct: He runs quickly.

Incorrect: She sings beautiful.

Correct: She sings beautifully.

However, some words can be adjectives or adverbs depending on the sentence:

- He is a fast runner.
- He runs fast.

---

## 55. Adverb Position

Adverbs can appear in different positions depending on the type of adverb and the sentence.

Examples:

- She **often** studies at night.
- He has **already** finished.
- They will **probably** arrive soon.
- She speaks English **fluently**.

### Exam trick

Check:

1. What does the adverb modify?
2. Is there an auxiliary verb?
3. Does the adverb have a conventional position?
4. Does moving it change the meaning?

---

## 56. MPT: Adverb Order

A commonly taught order for adverbs of manner, place and time is:

**Manner + Place + Time**

Examples:

- She worked **carefully** (manner) **in the laboratory** (place) **yesterday** (time).
- He spoke **politely** at the meeting this morning.

### Memory trick

**MPT = Manner → Place → Time**

This is a useful default for many exam sentences, but it is not an absolute rule for every possible adverbial construction.

---

## 57. Also, Too and As Well

All can express addition.

- She also speaks French.
- She speaks French, too.
- She speaks French as well.

`Also` usually appears before the main verb, after an auxiliary, or in another appropriate position.

- She **also works** here.
- She has **also completed** the task.

`Too` and `as well` commonly occur at the end.

### Exam trick

Do not assume that every one of these words must occupy exactly the same position.

---

## 58. Such, So and Too

### Such + noun phrase

- It was **such a difficult question**.
- She is **such a good teacher**.

### So + adjective/adverb

- The question was **so difficult**.
- He ran **so quickly**.

### So ... that

- It was so cold that we stayed inside.

### Too ... to

- He is too tired to work.

### Memory trick

- **Such + (a/an) + adjective + noun**
- **So + adjective/adverb**
- **Too + adjective/adverb + to + V1**

---

## 59. Enough

`Enough` generally follows an adjective or adverb but comes before a noun.

- She is old **enough** to drive.
- He runs quickly **enough**.
- We have **enough time**.

Incorrect: She is enough old.

Correct: She is old enough.

---

## 60. Since, Because, Because Of

- `Because` is followed by a clause.
- `Because of` is followed by a noun phrase or gerund.
- `Since` can indicate time or a reason, depending on context.

Examples:

- We stayed home because it was raining.
- We stayed home because of the rain.
- Since you are here, let us begin.
- I have lived here since 2020.

---

## 61. Question Tags

A question tag is a short question added to a statement.

### Basic pattern

Positive statement → negative tag

- You are ready, **aren't you?**
- She can swim, **can't she?**

Negative statement → positive tag

- He isn't ready, **is he?**
- They haven't arrived, **have they?**

### Special cases

- I am late, **aren't I?**
- Let's go, **shall we?**
- Open the door, **will you?**
- Nobody came, **did they?**

### Exam trick

Check the main clause's auxiliary, polarity and subject. Do not choose the tag by meaning alone.

---

## 62. There Is vs There Are

The verb agrees with the noun that follows.

- There **is** a book on the table.
- There **are** two books on the table.

For compound lists, agreement can depend on the first noun or the construction. In exam questions, identify the noun phrase being introduced and follow the expected agreement.

---

## 63. Subject–Verb Agreement with Quantifiers

Common structures:

- A number of students **are** absent.
- The number of students **is** increasing.
- Each of the students **has** a book.
- Many students **have** applied.
- Much of the work **is** complete.

### Exam trick

Compare:

- **A number of** = several → plural verb.
- **The number of** = the numerical total → singular verb.

---

## 64. Amount of vs Number of

- The amount of water **is** low.
- The number of books **is** high.

Do not confuse the noun inside the `of` phrase with the grammatical head of the subject.

---

## 65. The Verb “Enjoy”

`Enjoy` is normally followed by a noun or a gerund.

- I enjoy music.
- I enjoy reading.
- They enjoyed playing football.

Incorrect: I enjoy to read.

Correct: I enjoy reading.

---

## 66. Prefer, Enjoy and Similar Verb Patterns

Different verbs take different structures.

- enjoy + V-ing
- avoid + V-ing
- finish + V-ing
- suggest + V-ing / a clause
- decide + to V1
- hope + to V1
- want + to V1

Examples:

- She avoided **answering**.
- He decided **to leave**.
- I hope **to succeed**.

### Exam trick

Memorise the verb pattern, not just the meaning of the verb.

---

## 67. Earning vs Earnings

- **Earning** can function as a verb form or gerund.
- **Earnings** commonly refers to income or money earned.

Examples:

- She is earning a salary.
- His annual earnings have increased.

Use the form that matches the intended meaning and grammatical position.

---

## 68. Past Point of Time and Tense

A definite past-time expression usually points to the simple past.

- yesterday
- last week
- in 2022
- two hours ago

Examples:

- I saw him yesterday.
- She joined the school in 2022.

For a continuing situation extending to the present, the present perfect or present perfect continuous may be suitable:

- I have lived here for five years.
- I have been studying for two hours.

# PART 9: DSSSB TGT CS — ERROR DETECTION STRATEGY

When you see a sentence divided into four parts, check these in order:

1. **Subject–verb agreement:** Is the verb matching the real subject?
2. **Tense:** Is the time expression compatible with the tense?
3. **Pronoun:** Is the case correct—who/whom, he/him, they/them?
4. **Determiner:** Is it each/every, few/a few, little/a little?
5. **Conjunction:** Are paired expressions correctly matched?
6. **Preposition:** Is the correct preposition used after the word?
7. **Comparison:** Is the degree of the adjective correct?
8. **Verb pattern:** Does the verb take V-ing or to + V1?
9. **Article:** Is a/an/the needed or unnecessary?
10. **Word order:** Is the adverb in a suitable position?

### Time-saving trick

Do not read the sentence only for meaning. Read it once for meaning, then inspect its grammar in a fixed order.

A sentence may sound understandable but still contain a grammatical error.

# ANSWER KEY WITH EXPLANATIONS

1. **B — has.** Each of the + plural noun takes a singular verb.
2. **C — and.** The standard pair is both ... and.
3. **B — who.** Who is the subject of “helped.”
4. **C — whom.** The person is the object of “invited.”
5. **B — since.** 2021 is the starting point.
6. **C — than.** No sooner ... than.
7. **C — were.** Traditional hypothetical construction: If I were you.
8. **B — would have.** Third conditional: If + had + V3, would have + V3.
9. **C — reading.** Enjoy is followed by a gerund.
10. **C — to.** Prefer A to B.
11. **B — being.** Despite is followed by a noun or gerund phrase.
12. **C — fewer.** Mistakes are countable.
13. **B — as.** As ... as expresses equality.
14. **C — was.** The main subject is “teacher.”
15. **C — meeting.** Look forward to + V-ing.
16. **B.** Do not normally use although and but together in this structure.
17. **A — worth.** Worth + gerund: worth reading.
18. **B — fluently.** An adverb modifies “speaks.”
19. **B — ago.** Ago counts backwards from the present.
20. **C — is.** “The number” is singular.
